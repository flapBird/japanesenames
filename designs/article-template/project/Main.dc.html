J apanese names look simple in a textbook — a surname, a given name, a few kanji. In practice they are shaped by a family register law, an official kanji list, reading customs that are mostly free-form, and habits that go back to village life before the Meiji era.

This guide walks through the rules and the customs in plain English, so you can read any Japanese name — or choose one — with a clear picture of what is actually allowed and what is merely tradition.

1. The surname comes first

In Japanese, the family name (姓, sei) precedes the given name (名, mei). Tanaka Yuki is Ms. Tanaka, named Yuki — the reverse of the English order. Japanese documents, news media, and everyday speech all follow this order, and official Latin-alphabet passports have followed suit since 2020, when the government began recommending TANAKA YUKI over Yuki TANAKA in the machine-readable convention.

In Western contexts — business cards aimed at overseas partners, international bylines — many Japanese people simply reverse the order and accept the ambiguity. When you see a single name like "Sakura Suzuki," the safe reading is: Suzuki is the surname, Sakura the given name.

2. The given name is where the meaning lives

A Japanese given name is not a word with one fixed meaning. The sound — Haruto, Yui, Ren — is paired with a written form, and the written form carries the meaning. Haruto can be written 陽翔 (sun + soar), 悠斗 (leisurely + Big Dipper), or with dozens of other character pairs; each spelling is a different name with a different image.

This is why Japanese name discussions always start from the kanji. Two cousins both called Rin are not necessarily named after the same thing at all: one might be 凛 (dignified composure), another 琳 (a beautiful jewel).

3. Which kanji are legal?

You cannot write a Japanese name in any character you like. The Family Register Act limits name kanji to two official lists: the jōyō kanji — the 2,136 characters taught through high school — and the jinmeiyō kanji, an extra set of roughly 860 characters approved for personal names (things like 峻, 凛, and 葵). Hiragana, katakana, and long-vowel marks are also allowed, which is why purely kana names like さくら or メイ exist.

The list changes occasionally. Until a 2022 reform, kanji that denote numbers or positions (一, 二, 三, 今) were banned in names; the Ministry of Justice relaxed the rule. Characters considered violent or inappropriate — 汚 (filthy), 死 (death), 性 (sex) — remain off-limits, and a registrar can reject a written form outright.

4. Readings, however, are the wild west

Kanji choices are regulated; how you read them is not. Parents must register a reading (in kana) alongside the kanji, but the pairing can be remarkably creative.

Most parents stay conventional, but the loophole explains two things at once: why Japanese names resist automated guessing, and why a name should always be checked with the reading before you assume how it is pronounced.

5. Gendered endings — soft patterns, not rules

Nothing in the law marks a name as male or female, but phonetic patterns are strong. The recurring endings below read as feminine or masculine; a shared stock of unisex names sits in the middle, and its balance drifts by decade.

6. Families reuse characters across generations

Many families pass a single character through siblings or across generations, the way Western families reuse middle names. A first son might be 健太 (Kenta), his brothers 健吾 (Kengo) and 健二 (Kenji) — all sharing 健, the strong-and-healthy character. The character is called a 通字 (tsūji), and spotting it explains why some families' names feel rhymed.

7. No middle names — and one surname per household

  • Japan has no middle names. The register records exactly one surname and one given name.
  • The Civil Code requires a married couple to share a single registered surname; in practice roughly 95% of couples register under the husband's surname, though many women keep a maiden name in daily professional use (通称, tsūshō).
  • Foreigners who naturalize take a Japanese-registerable name; permanent residents keep their legal foreign name but often adopt a Japanese alias (通名) for local convenience.

8. Why there are so many surnames

Most Japanese surnames are small landscape maps: 高橋 (high bridge), 山本 (mountain base), 田中 (middle of the rice field), 清水 (clear water). Commoners needed permission to bear surnames through the Edo period, and when the Meiji government required every household to register one in 1875, villagers simply named themselves after what they could see.

9. Stroke-count fortune telling

A purely Japanese habit worth knowing: 姓名判断 (seimeihandan) scores a full name by the stroke count of its kanji, scoring combinations for fortune. It has no legal standing and no scientific basis, but it still influences real choices — some parents ask for the tally before registering.

10. What is trending right now

Recent annual surveys of newborn names show a consistent pattern: short, bright-sounding names with sun, light, or nature kanji top the lists — readings like Haruto, Ao, Himari, and Rin, each spread across several legal spellings. Questions and answers

Do Japanese names really come surname first?−

Yes. The family name precedes the given name in all Japanese-language contexts: Tanaka Yuki is surname Tanaka, given name Yuki. English-language contexts often reverse it, which is why international media show Yuki Tanaka.

Can any kanji be used in a Japanese name?+

No. Name kanji must come from the jōyō kanji (2,136 characters) plus the jinmeiyō kanji list of roughly 860 personal-name characters. Hiragana and katakana are also permitted. Registrars can reject written forms deemed inappropriate.

Is a name's meaning fixed by the sound?+

No. The meaning attaches to the kanji spelling, not the sound. Haruto written 陽翔 (sun + soar) and Haruto written 悠斗 are different names with different imagery that merely share a pronunciation.
